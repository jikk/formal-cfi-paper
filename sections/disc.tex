\pname takes a big step towards high assurance binary defense by formally verifying the binary rewriter, but it still trusts other components of the system.
Underlying the rewriter is the internal lifter, a manual implementation of the ARMv7 ISA semantics as a Gallina function translating code bytes to PIL statements.
Creating trustworthy mechanized semantics of ISAs is a subject of ongoing research~\cite{isla,islaris,archsem,armstrong19isasem,liblisa,goel17x86isa}

While \pname represents a big step towards high assurance binary defenses, there are still many weaknesses left to address.
First, the entire work rests on our trusted internal lifter, the Gallina function translating code bytes to PIL statements.
Verified and trustworthy lifting are subjects of ongoing research, but verifying PIL lifters is an open problem.
Second, the extraction is trusted.
The OCaml extractor is verified~\wc{trustworthy}, but in our extraction we extracted Rocq's unbounded integer type \texttt{Z} to OCaml's bounded \texttt{int}.
This opens the door to integer overflow and underflow bugs in the extracted code, but it makes the rewriter usable.
Extracting it without this optimization, that is an OCaml variant \texttt{Z} with the same structure as Rocq's, results in XXx longer rewrite times.
Rocq offers the capability of proving that this optimization is sound, i.e., that no over/underflows are possible, but we leave it to future work/industrial adoption.
Third, the ELF Packager is beyond the scope of formal verification because any specification of its correctness relies on the behavior of the loader.
To bring that part of the system into scope requires the availalability of a formally verified loader.

\subsection{Formal Verification of ELF Packager}\label{sec:elf_packager_fm}
\kjee{Tentative text; need refinement and text tightening.}

Although \pname{} is, to our knowledge, the first formally verified binary CFI rewriter, its guarantees do not yet extend to the entire binary rewriting pipeline. In particular, the ELF packaging component lies outside the scope of the current formal model. Extending formal verification to this stage presents nontrivial challenges and can be approached along two possible directions.

One approach is \emph{supply-chain verification}, which would require formally verifying the existing Python implementation or reimplementing the ELF manipulation logic (e.g., the LIEF library) within a proof assistant such as Rocq. However, this direction is likely impractical due to the substantial engineering effort required to formalize and maintain large, general-purpose codebases. A second, more ambitious direction is \emph{program loader verification}, which aims to formally verify the correctness of the binary loading process against a well-defined security specification. This approach would provide stronger end-to-end guarantees but remains a challenging open research problem.

More broadly, our current formalization focuses on the \texttt{.text} section, the executable instruction stream, and treats other components of the binary, including ELF metadata, relocation entries, and dynamic linking structures, as trusted. Extending formal guarantees to cover the full pipeline, including ELF packaging and loading, remains an important direction for future work.
