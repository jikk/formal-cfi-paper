\cfi has become a cornerstone defense against control-hijacking attacks, enabling widespread deployment with strong guarantees and near-native overhead. Yet the community focuses on design-level security guarantees, runtime overheads, and policy generation aspects, while largely overlooking the correctness of CFI implementations in production, leaving a critical gap between theory and practice.

This gap underscores a broader challenge in security engineering: translating sound designs into correct, deployable systems is difficult and costly. Even rigorously designed systems often contain subtle flaws that emerge only after deployment. In security-critical settings, these flaws do more than degrade functionality; they undermine the trusted computing base, create false confidence, and expose exploitable weaknesses.

To mitigate these risks, the community relies primarily on dynamic testing techniques such as fuzzing and symbolic execution. While effective at uncovering concrete bugs, these approaches remain inherently limited. This limitation stems not from engineering shortcomings, but from the fundamental nature of dynamic analysis.

\FMs take a fundamentally different approach. Rather than observing executions, formal methods establish stringent correctness guarantees by proving that an implementation satisfies formally specified properties, thereby covering all program behaviors within the scope of the specification. Proof assistants then mechanically check the resulting proof artifacts, reducing trust to the correctness of the specification and the soundness of the proof checker.

Despite these unparalleled advantages, formal methods have seen limited adoption in production systems for the following reasons:

Second, proofs bind tightly to specific implementations and verification contexts, making it difficult to scale verification across large, heterogeneous, and evolving codebases.

Binary-level CFI, built on static binary rewriting techniques, is a well-studied and mature area. Yet formally verifying the correctness of a binary CFI implementation, let alone the broader class of binary rewriters, remains an open challenge, despite the long-standing availability of mature proof assistants such as Coq/Rocq and Isabelle/HOL.

The core difficulty is one of semantic generality: correctness must hold for all possible input binaries, whether compiler-generated, hand-written, or otherwise, not merely those drawn from a particular toolchain or restricted to a subset of the ISA. Prior work has been largely confined to reasoning about concrete, fixed binaries, falling short of this bar. RockSalt~\cite{rocksalt}, for instance, successfully verified the safety guarantees of an SFI solution for x86, but its analysis is tied to a concrete subset of the x86 ISA and the NaCl sandbox policy, and does not extend to arbitrary binaries or other ISAs.

\optional{%
Formal verification directly targets this challenge by reasoning over all possible inputs rather than sampled executions, enabling universal correctness guarantees that dynamic testing cannot provide. These guarantees hold regardless of binary provenance, compiler, or ISA quirk, making formal methods particularly well suited to establishing that a CFI implementation soundly enforces its policy across the full space of binaries it may encounter.
}

We address the key obstacle, namely the absence of ISA abstractions amenable to formal reasoning, by building on the Picinae framework~\cite{picinae}, which supports machine-checked proofs in which the target binary is treated as a symbolic proof variable rather than a fixed program. This enables \pname to achieve verification that generalizes across arbitrary binaries, a capability no prior binary CFI rewriter has demonstrated.
